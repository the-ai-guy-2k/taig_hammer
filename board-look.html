2. Game data flow — one action, start to finish

Think of the game as a loop. The player does something, the game figures out the result, shows it, and waits for the next thing. Nothing happens by magic — data moves in a line.

Player picks an action → Game decides what kind of check it is → Roll a d20 (random 1 to 20) → Add the modifier (your skill bonus) → Compare total to the target number (DC) → Decide the outcome → Apply the effect → Update the game state → Show the result on screen → Back to: player picks the next action

1. Player picks an action

Example in original terms: “Search the wreck” or “Shoot the raider.” This is input. The game only knows what the player chose.

2. Game decides what kind of check it is

Search might be a perception-style check. Shooting might be an attack check. Each check points to one stat / modifier. No check, no roll.

3. Roll a d20

One random number, 1 to 20. Every face is equally likely. A natural 20 is a critical success, a natural 1 is a critical fail — before modifiers are even added.

4. Add the modifier

Total = roll + modifier. Example: roll 13 + modifier 3 = 16. The modifier is just how good your character is at that thing.

5. Compare total to the target number (DC)

If total is equal to or higher than the DC, you succeed. If lower, you fail. Example: DC 15, total 16 = success. DC 15, total 12 = fail. The DC is how hard the game decided this task is. It is not random.

6. Decide the outcome

Four buckets, keep them simple: Critical success (natural 20) / Success (total meets or beats DC) / Fail (total below DC) / Critical fail (natural 1). Later versions can add degrees of success. V1 does not need them.

7. Apply the effect

Success might mean: you find the salvage, you hit, the door opens. Fail might mean: you find nothing, you miss, you take damage, or the story moves a different way. The effect is the only place the story changes. The roll itself changes nothing until this step.

8. Update the game state

State is the small box of numbers and flags the game remembers between actions: health, ammo / supplies, position or scene, inventory, story flags (did you open the door? did the alarm go off?), roll log. If it is not in state, the game forgets it on the next action. That is the most common beginner bug, and the dashboard exists to catch it.

9. Show the result on screen

Show, in this order so it is checkable: what you tried, what you rolled, your modifier, your total, the DC, and the outcome. If a player cannot see those six things, they cannot trust the roll — and neither can a tester.

10. Loop

Back to step 1, until the mission ends (win, lose, or retreat) or the player quits. A game is mostly this loop, repeated. Missions, levels, and stories are just different actions and effects plugged into the same loop.

Data flow, as boxes

[ Screen / Buttons ] -- choice --> [ Check picker ] -- check type --> [ Dice roller ] -- roll --> [ Modifier + DC compare ] -- outcome --> [ Effect ] -- changes --> [ Game state ] -- display --> [ Screen / Log ]

Every arrow is one handoff. If a step is skipped, the next step gets bad data. When something looks wrong in testing, walk the arrows backwards: was the state wrong, the DC wrong, the modifier wrong, or the roll wrong?

3. How the game is played — in plain words

You play one person trying to get a job done in a rough, broken future. It is just you, solo. No team online, no one else playing against you.

How a turn works: The game tells you where you are and what is in front of you. You pick what to do — search, talk, sneak, shoot, fix something. The game rolls a 20-sided die, adds how good you are at that thing, and checks it against how hard the thing is. Then it tells you plainly what happened, and you pick the next move.

Your main objective: Finish the job and get out alive. For the first short mission, that means one clear job, not five at once — for example, search a wreck and bring the salvage back. If you finish the job and make it out, you win the run.

How you lose: If your health runs out, or the job falls apart beyond fixing, the run ends. You can try again — the dice will fall differently next time.

Side goals, not required to win: Find useful supplies, keep your health and gear in decent shape, and do not waste everything on the first fight. They make the next run easier, but they are not the win itself.

What the dice are for: They are luck plus skill. A good character still can roll badly, and a weak character still can roll well. That is the point — you make smart choices, the dice decide how well they land.

You do not need to learn rules first. Every result shows what you tried, what you rolled, your bonus, the target number, and whether it worked.
